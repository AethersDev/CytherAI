How it was made

This is a rendering, made in Blender with Cycles on an Apple M4, and every fringe in it is computed, not drawn. For each frame, each point of each block asks what an optical flat would show there: the reflectance of the stack of glass, a 15-nanometre silver film, the air gap and the steel, every reflection inside it counted, for the two yellow lines of sodium, at the gap the scene sets at that instant, its phase taken at the camera’s own angle, through its own lens. The reflectances themselves are computed at normal incidence, an approximation for a camera that looks down at an angle, and the steel is given iron’s constants.

The flats are partially silvered, as the flats of gauge interferometers were, which sharpens the fringes. Each one lands, presses out its film of air and comes to rest on a slight wedge; the wedge sets how far apart the lines lie. The second block’s rise is a smooth dome 1.2 micrometres high. No fringe was placed by hand.

The optical constants are published measurements: fused silica (Malitson, 1965), iron (Johnson and Christy, 1974) and silver (Johnson and Christy, 1972), from the refractiveindex.info database. Two independent ways of computing the stack, the Airy sum and characteristic matrices, give the same reflectance for an unsilvered flat, between 0.382 and 0.640. That agreement, like this page’s with the film, checks the computation, not the model.

What it is not

A rendering, not a photograph. The blocks, the dome and the wedges are ours; the physics is not. In the scene the flats are perfect, so straight, parallel, evenly spaced lines mean the first block is flat; in a real inspection they mean it is flat to within the reference flat’s own accuracy. A real inspection would also meet dust, warmth and the hand that lays the glass.
